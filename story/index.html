Small machines tell the truth.

A kid, a Pentium 4, and the long way to simulated nervous systems

2010 to 2026 · about 44 minutes to read

This is how I got from typing cheat codes on someone else's computer (2010, extremely serious business) to building compilers, GPU kernels, and simulated nervous systems. In between there is a test I failed twice, a stretch with no computer at all, a year of building things I did not believe in, and three months where I wrote nothing. If you draw the path, it zigzags. But if you squint at it, the same few ideas keep showing up at every stop, which is either a deep pattern or a sign that I only know about four ideas. Possibly both.

Part 1

Someone else's computer

2010 to 2013

The first computer I remember using was not mine, which was the perfect setup: all of the wonder, none of the responsibility. It was 2010, I was at a friend of a friend's house, and on the screen was Grand Theft Auto: Vice City. At some point he leaned over, typed a random word on the keyboard while the game was running, and a car fell out of the sky in front of us.

Just dropped out of nowhere, engine running, ready to drive. Cheat codes. I remember staring at that car like it had violated a law of physics. In a sense, it had.

His physics, not mine.

Something in my brain rewired itself that day. A cheat code leaks a secret about the world you are looking at: there are two sets of rules. There is the surface set, the ones the game shows you (to get a car, go find one, or steal one, like everyone else). And underneath, there is the real set, where a car is just an object sitting in the program's memory, and the program can put that object anywhere it wants, anytime it wants, if the right word gets typed.

I did not have any of this vocabulary then. I was a kid watching a car fall out of the sky. But I walked away with a feeling: typing the right words can change a world, and I would like to know more words, please.

Around the same age, my brain was running a very simple model of how the world works. The model went something like: if you want a thing, and you are smart about getting it, you mostly get it. Effort plus intelligence equals stuff. Then I started noticing data that did not fit the model. I kept meeting kids who were, and I say this with all the confidence of a child doing peer evaluation, no smarter than me, who had the exact things I wanted.

The difference was not effort. The difference was that their families could just buy the things. Written down, this sounds obvious, and it is. But as a kid it is a genuine plot twist. It means your little equation is missing its biggest variable, and nobody at school ever writes that variable on the board.

Meanwhile, at school, I was not what you would call a good student. I was more of a small, distractible question machine. My main academic activity was pointing at words and asking what they meant. I remember asking my computer teacher what a hyperlink was, because I had spotted the word sitting in a menu in Microsoft Excel and it bothered me that I was using a program packed with words I could not define.

She told me. And that was basically the shape of all my early learning: unknown word spotted in the wild, walk up to nearest patient adult, ask, receive definition, repeat. It is not a system exactly, but it got me surprisingly far.

Then in 2011 a friend accidentally upgraded my operating system. He showed me that he studied on his own, at home, before anyone had taught him the material in class. Until that moment I believed, in my bones, that learning worked like a car: nothing moves until a teacher shows up and turns the key. The idea that I could just sit down with a book and start the engine myself was new information.

So I tried it. And suddenly I had my first good year at school: strong in English, Hindi, and French, strong in music (the music teacher treated me like I was better than the rest of the class, and I was in no hurry to correct her), and even good at mathematics, although to be fair that was mostly arithmetic at that point.

I never stopped doing that friend's trick. Almost everything later in this story, the compilers, the GPU kernels, all of it, is just self-study pointed at scarier material.

In 2012 my cousin introduced me to HTML, and my setup was extremely professional: his computer, his book, and me hovering nearby writing pages in Notepad++ while he prepared to become a web developer. I also tried to learn PHP at the same time, which mostly involved me not knowing what PHP did. I spent a long, long time trying to install Zend Server.

The problem was that I was following setup steps toward a destination I could not picture at all, like assembling furniture for a room I had never seen. What I did not know then is that this is actually the normal first phase of learning almost any tool. Phase one is copying the motions with total confusion. The understanding shows up later, uninvited, and explains what your hands were doing the whole time.

And copying was really the whole method. I built almost nothing from scratch. I would find a website I liked, open its source code, and steal bits of it. I took templates from sites like W3Layouts and SourceCodester, which I adored, and which loaded at the speed of continental drift. Getting on the internet at all meant connecting a Nokia phone to the computer over Bluetooth using Nokia PC Suite and sipping data through its GPRS connection.

When the internet arrives that slowly, you stop treating it like air and start treating it like well water: you go fetch exactly what you need, you carry it home, you save it, and you work offline. In that environment, View Source became my first real textbook, because every web page secretly ships with a complete explanation of how it was made.

You just have to be willing to read someone else's markup line by line, which, at GPRS speeds, you have plenty of time to do.

By 2013 there was a computer at home, a very slow Pentium 4, and it was less a tool than a set of terms and conditions. Everything happened on its schedule. I played Age of Empires II for hours. I played Max Payne and a big pile of old DOS games. I messed around with modifications for GTA and made little Flash animations.

And I downloaded the books I desperately wanted to learn from, these big O'Reilly volumes on HTML, CSS, PHP, and JavaScript, and then sat there watching Adobe Reader fail to render their pages. A book about how to make pages. On a computer that could not open pages. The universe has a sense of humor and it was doing material.

In those years I used the computer the way you use a calculator: switch it on for a specific purpose, do the thing, switch it off, go live your life. The idea that a person would just be connected to the internet all day, every day, as a default state of being, had not arrived yet.

Opening a heavy program on that machine was a full sensory event. The fan would spin up like it was preparing for takeoff. The cursor would freeze. The disk would make these frantic little sounds. You could literally hear software load. Now think about what two different kids learn from two different machines.

A kid with a fast computer learns that programs are free: you click, window appears, magic. A kid with a slow computer learns that every program has a price and the currency is his own waiting. Every extra process, every background updater quietly doing who knows what, every careless megabyte some developer did not think about, all of it showed up as seconds of my life that I was never getting back.

At the time I did not experience any of this as a lesson. I experienced it as: this computer is bad and I would like a better one. But it installed a reflex in me that is still running today, like a tiny background process of my own. Before I ask what a piece of software does, I ask what it costs to run. Most people almost never need to ask that question, because their machines pay the bill silently. On a Pentium 4, you ask it every single day, out loud, at the screen.

Part 2

The test

2014 to 2016, and after

In 2014 I joined an institute about two kilometres from home. Two kilometres is a nothing distance to me now, I walk it without thinking. Back then it was officially The Far Away Place, the farthest I had ever been allowed to go on my own, and going there meant that for the first time, my own safety outside the house was my job.

The reason that year worked was the teacher. He did something sneaky, which is that instead of just pouring information into me, he installed habits: take notes like you are thinking on paper, prepare every topic as if someone is about to cross-examine you on it. I started setting question papers for myself, for my own study, and I took that seriously.

That little habit matters more than it sounds. When you write a question, you are forced to find the exact spot where a topic can be misunderstood, the trapdoor in it. When you merely answer questions, you only have to find the path through. Question-writers and question-answerers are looking at the same topic from opposite ends, and the writer's end teaches you more.

In 2015 I started coaching classes with the energy of a person who is about to take over the world. I would love to report that the world-taking went well. It did not. The first couple of months were fine, and then the enjoyment just drained out of it. I was socially anxious around the other students, and I was tired in a way that sleep did not seem to fix, because the commute itself was a daily expedition: walking miles in hot summer heat to get there and then walking miles back.

I still spent my time around the teachers, asking them things, partly because I had questions and partly because teachers were simply easier to be around. My school physics teacher was superb, and watching him taught me something I only had words for much later: that clear communication and stubborn optimism from a teacher can actually change the list of things a student believes he is allowed to attempt.

I hated chemistry. I want that on the record. I hated it with everything I had. But I loved solving problems, and mathematics, which was hard for me, got whole weeks of my life, just practising questions and basically nothing else. The focus reached levels where I sometimes forgot to comb my hair, and at least once arrived having forgotten my shoes. I present the shoes as a data point.

Draw your own graph.

So why would a kid pour himself into subjects like that? The obvious answer is ambition, and ambition is real, but it is only half the story. The other half is this: subjects were the one place where the rules stayed put. Physics did not care where I was from. A mathematics problem did not quietly recalibrate itself based on my accent or my name.

A chess problem gave back exactly the attention I put into it, no more, no less, like a vending machine for fairness. Out in the human world, the rules seemed to change depending on who you were, which is a confusing and exhausting way to live. Inside a subject, same rules, every day, for everyone. So I started spending more and more of my time inside my own head.

From the outside it probably just looked like a boy who really liked learning, and that was true. It was also the one room where I got to control the guest list.

There is a trick you learn when you are in a situation you cannot physically leave: you leave in the other direction. Inward. Your eyes stay open. Your mouth still produces answers when spoken to. But the part of you that pays attention quietly relocates to a quieter neighborhood, and whatever is happening to you arrives late and muffled, like news about somebody else.

I got good at this trick. Then I got so good at it that I stopped noticing I was doing it, the way you stop noticing you are breathing. Years later I would catch myself watching my own life like a film about a stranger: fully aware of the plot, weirdly unable to feel any of it. And there is a second piece of kid logic that pairs with this.

When a child is hurting and nobody explains why, the child writes his own explanation, and the explanation almost always points at himself. This sounds like a bug but it is actually a strategy, because self-blame is the only theory that comes with a to-do list. If the adults are the problem, or the world is, then a kid can do exactly nothing.

But if I am the problem, well, maybe I can fix me: be better, be quieter, be smaller, take up less space. Blaming yourself hurts. It also hands you instructions.

That is why I carried it around for so long.

And then there was the test. I will not name it, because you already know the genre: the one examination that everyone around you describes as The Door, the single entrance into a completely different life. I believed them. My family believed them. Money that could not easily be spared got spent. Years went in.

And I failed it. Twice. The subjects I loved did not ride in and save me, and looking back, the reason is almost funny in its simplicity: loving a subject and performing in that subject on one fixed morning, in one fixed format, under one fixed clock, are two completely different skills, and the test was only ever measuring the second one.

Nobody had mentioned that distinction. For a while after the second failure I considered not continuing at all, and I mean that in the largest sense. I stayed. If you want the single dramatic reason, there is not one, and I have stopped being embarrassed about that. Part of it was stubbornness. Part of it was a plain, animal curiosity about what happens next.

At that age, it turns out that was enough.

Here is the conclusion I eventually crawled to, slowly, over years. The test was making a much bigger claim than any test can honestly make. It was not just reporting a score, it was issuing a verdict on what a person was worth attempting. But a test can only measure how well someone takes that test, on that day, in that format.

Everything beyond that is an inference, and in my case it was a lousy inference wearing a suit. So the question became: if that measurement is broken, what would a better measurement look like? And the answer I landed on was almost embarrassingly direct. Make actual things. Put them in front of strangers.

Let the strangers judge the thing sitting in front of them, which they will, because they know nothing about your numbers and their browser does not ask for your rank before loading a page. I stopped waiting around to be measured correctly by the official measuring machine, and started producing evidence of a different kind.

The first pieces of evidence were small enough to be embarrassing. Good. Small and real beats large and hypothetical every time, and if this story has a rule it keeps reusing, that is the rule.

Part 3

Programming on a phone

2017

The next chapter of the plan had a small logistical problem: I wanted to program, and I did not have a computer. What I had was a phone. So, phone it was. This is actually a great way to find out which parts of programming are essential and which parts are just furniture. Turns out you need: somewhere to type, somewhere to run the thing, and stubbornness. Everything else is furniture.

In 2017 I started writing Telegram bots on that phone. The first bot replied to messages with random quotes, took about an hour to build, and was deployed using a state-of-the-art pipeline that went like this: SSH from the phone into a $5 server, type python bot.py, press enter, done. That was the entire DevOps department. One guy, one phone, one command.

Now, a bot is a funny kind of program because its whole job is to keep existing after you stop paying attention to it. You put the phone down, you go to sleep, and the bot is supposed to sit there being a bot. This is a lovely idea right up until reality gets involved, because processes die. Mine died at 3am, from timeouts, from garbage input sent by creative strangers, from running out of memory. And every time, there I was, half asleep, fixing production code on the same tiny phone screen I had written it on.

Character-building is the polite phrase.

Do that enough times and the question you are asking starts to mutate. In the beginning the question is: how do I stop this thing from dying? Eventually, usually around the tenth 3am incident, a better question shows up: okay, given that it will die, what should happen when it does? My answer was to move everything important out of the process itself.

The queue of work, the cache, the log of what already happened, all of that lives on disk, outside any single run. Now when the process dies at 3am, the damage report says: restart it. The new process wakes up, reads the log to find out where its predecessor dropped everything, picks the work back up, and nobody loses a night of messages. The process became replaceable.

The state became the thing you protect.

I did not know it at the time, but that 3am phone lesson was going to follow me around for years. I keep relearning it at bigger and bigger scales, and it shows up again near the end of this story wearing much fancier clothes, attached to software that is a long way from a quote bot.

Part 4

Four gigabytes

ThinkPad E480, Linux

Later I got a ThinkPad E480 with 4 GB of RAM, and I want to do the math out loud because the math is the whole story. If your operating system eats about 2 GB just sitting there existing, that leaves roughly 2 GB for the browser, the editor, and whatever you are actually trying to build. Three large animals, one small watering hole.

So I moved to Linux. People assume that kind of switch is about philosophy or taste. Mine was about arithmetic. I needed those gigabytes back and Linux was willing to negotiate.

Here is something I have come to believe: necessity is a much stricter teacher than curiosity, because curiosity lets you quit when you get bored and necessity just stands there with its arms crossed. I cycled through one Linux distribution after another. I stripped out services I did not need and then sat there watching memory numbers in a terminal like a day trader, checking what each removal bought me.

And somewhere in that process I learned how an operating system is actually put together, using my favorite method: take pieces of it out and see what complains.

I also switched to a tiling window manager,1 and my whole screen became exactly three panes: the file I was editing, the test output, and my notes. That layout sounds like a preference but it functioned like a discipline. Three panes means three things in your head at a time.

If the change I was making could not fit into three panes, that was the machine gently informing me that I was doing two jobs at once and doing both of them worse.

So the ThinkPad's big lesson was about invitations. Every tool you install is a guest you invite onto the machine, and guests in software are easy to invite and remarkably hard to evict. Each one wants a little memory, a little screen, a little of your attention, forever. On a machine with memory to burn, you never notice the guests piling up. On 4 GB, you notice every single one of them, by name, because they are all standing in your kitchen.

Part 5

Search before it had a name

2018 to 2020

A humbling and useful discovery from this period: a Linux laptop and a $5 server are, together, enough for one person to build and run real things, start to finish, no permission slip required. So for the next few years, that is what I did. Just a long series of small systems that had to actually work, because real people were on the other end of them and real people are undefeated at finding the one input you did not test.

In 2018 I rewrote my Python bots in Node.js, and this is where I met nested callbacks, a phenomenon I can only describe as architectural quicksand. Each individual callback looks totally innocent when you write it. Sure, put this inside that. And this inside that. And then one day you zoom out and your code is a staircase marching off the right edge of the screen, and your errors are quietly falling through the gaps between the steps, never to be seen again.

Nobody designs that mess on purpose. It accumulates, one reasonable decision at a time, which is how most messes in life happen.

Between 2019 and 2020, something pulled me sideways into information retrieval, which is the unglamorous name for a beautiful idea: how do you find the thing you mean inside a giant pile of things? I indexed Telegram archives in discover, built a quiet little book search interface in shoten, and started experimenting with vectors, embeddings, and HNSW nearest-neighbor search2 in sentence-similarity-search.

This was before the whole industry started calling this stuff AI, so to me it just looked like common sense wearing math. Here is the idea, and it is genuinely simple. Take a piece of text and turn it into a point in space. Do that for every piece of text you have. Now your pile of text is a map. When someone asks a question, turn the question into a point too, drop it on the map, and look at which points are standing closest to it. Those are your answers. Meaning becomes geography. I never really got over how neat that is.

From discover, shoten, and sentence-similarity-search. Most of the points never get looked at.

Alongside the search experiments I was running automated Telegram deal and price-tracking pipelines, and these had a specific kind of pressure: real traffic. Real traffic has a defining personality trait, which is that it does not pause politely while you fix things. It keeps arriving, at 3am, on weekends, during your lunch.

So my phone-era rule came back, this time with actual consequences attached: state lives outside the process, a restart picks up exactly where the last run stopped, and a crash is an inconvenience instead of a small funeral.

Part 6

Three quiet months

2021

In 2021 I burned out and wrote nothing for three months.

I wish I had an interesting dispatch from inside those three months, some dramatic before-and-after, but honestly the most accurate report is: it was quiet, and then I came back clearer. Sometimes the update is just that the system needed to be off.

A full stop like that does do one useful thing, though, and it is worth naming. When you slow down gradually, you can drift back into work on pure momentum, like a shopping cart rolling across a parking lot. When you stop completely, momentum is gone. To restart, you have to physically pick each thing back up, one at a time, on purpose.

And when you are standing there looking at all the things you dropped, you realize some of them can just stay on the floor. They were never yours. You were just carrying them because you had never once put everything down.

Part 7

The detour

2023 to 2024

In 2023 I picked up Rust to rewrite my command-line tools. Rust's reputation for being difficult is earned, but the difficulty has a very specific shape. Most languages are like a friend who says: go ahead, build the thing, we will figure out the problems when they explode. Rust is like a building inspector who lives in your editor and refuses to let the first version even compile until you answer a list of questions: who owns this value?

What error can this call return? How long does this reference live, exactly, in writing please? Most languages let you discover those answers in production, at 3am, in front of an audience. Rust makes you settle them at your desk, in daylight, like an adult. Given my history with 3am, I considered this an excellent trade.

Then in 2024 I ran an experiment on myself. I decided to try doing what you are supposed to do, the standard, sensible, everyone-on-the-internet-agrees playbook: Nuxt and Vercel AI wrappers, content-repurposing pipelines, autonomous agent templates, DeFi liquidation bots, and smart-contract bug bounties with immunefi-cli.

On paper, all of it was smart. All of it was where the energy and the money supposedly were. I wanted to see what happened if I just, for once, followed the map everyone else was using.

The experiment worked perfectly, in the sense that it produced a very clear result: I did not care about any of it. And that was useful information, the way a failed experiment is useful because it tells you where the treasure is not buried. Gluing heavy JavaScript stacks onto other people's remote APIs felt fragile, because every layer belonged to someone else and could change or vanish under me at any moment, like building a house on a series of rented trampolines.

And it felt hollow, because on the days when it worked, I could not point at the screen and say: that part, that part is mine.

The work that did feel real that year came from the opposite direction. Finding an actual replay bug in a protocol meant sitting down with the raw source code and checking every invariant myself, line by line, no shortcuts. It asked for exactly the same thing that keeping a bot alive at 3am had asked for years earlier: you do not get to treat anything as a black box.

Not the protocol, not the process, not your own assumptions. And it hit me that this was the thing I had been looking for the whole time. I want work where I am expected, required even, to understand all of it. Which sounds like a nice preference until you realize it quietly rules out most of the modern software industry.

Part 8

Opening the boxes

Compilers, files, hardware

So I stopped building wrappers on top of other people's boxes, and started doing the opposite: opening boxes. You know the boxes I mean. The big, sealed, official pieces of infrastructure that everyone treats as finished and slightly sacred, like you are not really supposed to ask what is inside. I developed one simple test for any tool that felt bloated or mysterious.

The test is a question: if I threw away all the framework around this thing, how many lines would the actual mechanism take? Not the product.

The mechanism.

And the answer, over and over, was: way, way fewer than I expected. Here is the mental picture I now carry around. At the center of most software there is a small, bright idea, something genuinely clever that fits on an index card. Around that idea, like rings around a tree, are years of accumulated everything-else: compatibility layers, configuration options, special cases for situations you will personally never encounter.

All of that outer material serves somebody, and I am not saying it should not exist. I am saying that if your goal is to understand the thing, you are allowed to set the rings aside and look at the center. And the center, very often, fits in a single file that you can read in one sitting.

Compilers were the first boxes I pried open, because compilers had the biggest reputation for being magic. I wrote a C compiler from first principles in sectorc, a Zig implementation of the interaction-calculus runtime in hvm, self-hosting compilers for Arturo, J, and Bend 2, a tracing JIT for Wren in WrenJIT, an array-scheduling operating system in tacit, and minimal virtual machines for Erlang's BEAM (beam.zig) and TON (zig-tvm). Yes, that is a lot of boxes. Once you start it is hard to stop.

It is bubble wrap for a very specific kind of nerd.

And here is the anticlimactic secret about compilers: a compiler looks like magic right up until you write one. Then it looks like a series of translations, each one small enough and boring enough that you can check it by hand. Magic, it turns out, is usually just a lot of unglamorous steps standing in a trench coat.

Machine learning got the same treatment, because if anything on Earth currently wears a bigger magic costume than compilers, it is neural networks. I wrote reverse-mode automatic differentiation on plain NumPy in nabla, a neural network in roughly 600 lines of Zig in nn.zig, and a fast BPE tokenizer core in Rust in blaze-bpe.

And when you strip a neural network down to the studs like that, the mystery evaporates and what is left standing there is: multiplication, addition, and the chain rule from calculus, run backwards. That is the whole animal.

The magic was the trench coat again.

Then I turned to the most boring-sounding category of all, everyday files, which is exactly why it was interesting. tinypdf creates real PDFs in under 400 lines of TypeScript with zero dependencies. zpdf does zero-copy SIMD text extraction in Zig.

zs3 is S3 storage with no dependencies, and walsync streams SQLite write-ahead logs across machines. PDFs! The file format everyone assumes requires a corporation and a subscription. Under 400 lines.

The trench coats are everywhere.

Then the box-opening habit escaped the computer and got into physical hardware, which is where it stops being a philosophy and starts being a personality trait. I bought a Brother HL-L2440DW laser printer, looked at its driver situation, and thought: how hard can the actual mechanism be? So I reverse-engineered its compressed 1-bit raster format and wrote a 400-line C program (brother-hl-l2440dw) that prints over raw TCP, no Brother driver involved. Me, my printer, and a socket.

Direct eye contact.

Because here is what a printer driver actually is, underneath the furniture-like officialness of it: it is a format. Rows of black and white dots, compressed in a particular way, sent down a wire in a particular order. That is it. Once you know the format, the driver is a short program that a single person can write and, more importantly, a single person can understand all the way through. A lot of intimidating infrastructure is just a format wearing a suit and hoping you will not ask questions.

The same pattern kept firing. When I was fighting a dead programmer on an AI-Thinker ESP32-CAM, I wrote self-contained MJPEG streaming firmware (esp32-cam-web-streamer) that serves video straight to any browser on the local network, no cloud account, no app, no phoning home to a server on another continent just so I can see a camera that is in my own house.

And when I wanted a 3D-printed part to actually fit an irregular opening on the first print instead of the fourth, I built reality-fit, which goes from a 10-second webcam scan next to a paper marker to a fitted mesh.

Small irritations, small mechanisms, no middlemen.

If I zoom out and look at all of these projects at once, every single one started with the same tiny splinter of irritation: a tool that wanted more from me than the job actually required. More dependencies, more accounts, more trust, more megabytes. And every single time I actually checked, the job underneath turned out to be smaller than the tool wrapped around it. I do not think this pattern is ever going to stop being true, which means I have a hobby for life.

Part 9

The copy tax

Apple Silicon and WebGPU, 2024 onward

In 2024 I moved my daily work onto Apple Silicon, and this quietly deleted one of the oldest taxes in computing. Picture the traditional setup: your CPU and your GPU live in two different houses, each with its own memory. Every time the GPU needs to work on something, the data has to pack a suitcase, cross the street, get processed, and cross back.

That round trip is the copy tax, and for years everyone just paid it because that was the geography. Unified memory changes the geography completely: the CPU and GPU now share one pool of RAM,3 same house, same fridge. Which means a 14-inch laptop can hold model weights and run work that used to require renting a remote server, and the data never has to travel anywhere.

And my old 4 GB ThinkPad reflex immediately kicked in, because this is exactly the shape of assumption I was trained to poke. A huge amount of received wisdom in computing is just old hardware facts that nobody went back to re-check after the hardware changed. Everyone kept saying certain work needs a cluster. Needs it why, exactly? Because it needed it in 2016? That is not a law of physics, that is an expired observation.

So I checked, in the most direct way available, which is to just try the forbidden thing and watch what happens. I built a CUDA compiler and runtime for Apple Silicon GPUs in cuda-metal. Metal GPUs do not have hardware double precision, which sounds like a deal-breaker until you emulate it, which I did in VF64-metal.4 And once that door was open, the serious scientific software started walking through it: GROMACS (gromacs-metal), WarpX plasma simulations (warpx-metal), PeleLMeX combustion (pele-metal), and Box3D physics (box3d-metal), all running on the M-series GPU inside a laptop. A laptop. The cluster turned out to be optional, which nobody had mentioned.

Language and speech models got the same interrogation. I wrote fused Metal kernels in Zig with no Python and no PyTorch in the loop (tiny-kernel), ran a 2-bit 27B model locally at 20 tokens per second (bonsai-mlx), and then pushed the whole idea to its logical extreme by putting full speech recognition (phonon-web) and a language model (barunlm-35m-browser) entirely inside the browser, on WebGPU and WebAssembly. A browser tab. The place where you check the weather is now running a speech recognizer.

And notice how this is the exact photographic negative of the 2024 wrapper year. Back then, everything I built lived on someone else's server and died at their convenience. Now the audio never leaves the machine, the model never leaves the machine, and the whole thing keeps working even if some company's API team has a bad Tuesday. The copy tax was never just about copies. It was about how much of your own work you actually own.

Part 10

Where the work is going

Now

After enough years of opening boxes, something interesting happened to my questions: they changed subjects. It stopped being only about how small a mechanism could be, and started being about what kinds of things are even worth building mechanisms for. When I look at almost everything I am working on now, I can see three threads running through it, like three cables braided together. Let me untangle them.

Learning that can be checked

Thread one is a suspicion about how learning should work. The current fashion is brute force: make the transformer bigger, feed it more internet, spend more money, and out pops something smart-ish that will also, cheerfully and with total confidence, invent a plausible-sounding lie right to your face.

I keep staring at that picture and thinking there has to be a less insane route. So I have been exploring learning through compression, search, and proof instead of scale. The intuition is simple enough to say out loud: if a learner is forced to compress what it sees, it cannot cheat by memorizing, because memorizing costs too many bits.5 To get smaller, it has to find the actual pattern.

And if an answer arrives with a proof attached, something a machine can check, then you do not need to trust whoever or whatever produced it. Trust gets replaced by checking, and checking scales in a way that trust never will.

So, concrete experiments. In solon I built a language learner around Minimum Description Length with no transformer, no gradients, and no backpropagation, just to see how far pure compression can walk on its own when data is scarce. In supsearch, ontogenesis, and progen, program synthesis engines solve benchmarks and invent their own reusable lambda-calculus vocabulary without calling a language model at all, like watching a mind build its own words because it got tired of spelling everything out.

And in gavel, reinforcement learning policies are scored by Bend's proof checker instead of by a learned reward model, because a learned reward model can be gamed, flattered, and fooled, and a proof checker cannot. A proof checker is the one judge with no ego to appeal to.

That same obsession with checkable truth runs through open-magnetar, a post-quantum RV64I zero-knowledge VM over binary fields that uses only standard hashes, and claim-wire, an incremental compiler that treats every number in a Markdown document as a typed build target that can be traced back to the exact run that produced it.

That last one might be my favorite expression of the whole thread, because think about how weird our current situation is. In a program, every value has a paper trail. In a document, a number can just appear, fully formed, with no parents and no history, and everyone nods. Why? A number in a document should be exactly as checkable as a function in a program.

Anything less is just typography asking for trust.

Biology and physics as programs

Thread two starts with a question that sounds like science fiction and is actually an engineering plan: what happens if you compile a living thing into code? Not metaphorically. Literally take a biological or physical system, turn it into a runnable, differentiable simulation, and then treat it the way programmers treat programs.

Run it. Inspect it. Change one variable and see what breaks. Biology stops being a thing you only observe from the outside and becomes a thing you can poke from the inside, as fast as your curiosity can type.

The biggest poke so far is fly-brain, where the complete connectome of a fruit fly, all 165,122 neurons and 104 million synapses of it, runs as a spiking brain inside a physics-simulated fly body, in a browser. Sit with that for a second. An entire animal's wiring diagram, alive-ish, in a browser tab.

And it lets you ask a question that is very easy to state and very hard to answer any other way: how much of what a fly does comes purely from the wiring, from the diagram itself, and how much needs extra rules that live outside the graph? You cannot ask the fly. Well, you can, but the fly is not a reliable narrator.

The worms got it next. In worm-sim, occam-worm, and molecular-compiler, connectomes and molecular profiles of C. elegans are compiled into differentiable simulations that go hunting for compact local neural update rules, small simple laws that can predict how real animals, animals the model has never seen, will respond when you intervene.

And the same compile-the-world instinct keeps spreading: whole E. coli cells (life-sim), magnetic nozzles and tokamak plasma (helicon, driftmap, plasma-tsu-tomo), drone swarms that have to navigate without GPS (skein), urban microclimates (urbancool-jax), and the slow death of lithium-ion batteries (BatteryUDE.jl). Different systems, same move: turn it into a program, then interrogate the program.

Agents inside deterministic walls

Thread three is about autonomous software, and it is basically my 2017 phone lesson coming back for its final form. I build agent environments (helium, carry-terminal, mimo-build), capability sandboxes (caplock), declarative deployment engines (alchemy-celld), and self-running media and commerce pipelines.

And every one of them obeys the same constitutional law, adjusted for a new kind of citizen: never let a probabilistic step hold state or custody. The thing that improvises does not get to be the thing that remembers, and it definitely does not get to be the thing that decides.

Because here is the realization that ties the whole story in a knot. A language model is, operationally, a bot process at 3am. It will usually do the right thing. Sometimes it will not, and you will not be awake when that happens, and it will be very confident while doing the wrong thing, which is worse than a crash in some ways, because at least a crash is honest.

So the architecture writes itself: the model proposes, deterministic code decides. Deterministic code owns the log (memo), the grammar (typed-inference), the permissions, and the verification gate at the door.

When the model is wrong, and it will be wrong, the wrongness stops at the gate, the damage stays small, and the log sits there afterward calmly explaining exactly what happened, like a flight recorder that has seen some things. Same lesson as the phone. Bigger bot. Higher stakes.

Same rule.

Part 11

Coming back

Walking, and after

At some point in the middle of all this building, I started walking. I do not mean that metaphorically, and I do not mean a lap around the block to clear my head. I mean actual, literal walking, across cities, up mountains, over state lines, mostly alone, for years. The photographs in the gallery are the leftovers.

For a long time I told myself I was looking for something specific out there, and maybe I was. But I think I understand it better now: a huge part of the point was the motion itself. I had spent a childhood in rooms I could not leave. As an adult, I apparently needed to collect a large amount of evidence, gathered on foot, town by town, that I could leave any room, any place, whenever I wanted, under my own power, with nobody able to call me back.

Some facts you can be told. This one I had to prove to myself a few hundred times. The walls had stopped being walls, and my feet were the peer review.

Mountains, it turns out, are excellent company for this kind of project, mostly because they want absolutely nothing from you. A mountain does not care where you are from. It does not care how you did on an exam in 2016. And silence at altitude is not a prize handed out for good performance, it is just there, unconditionally, for whoever is willing to walk up into it.

This was genuinely new information for my nervous system. I had mostly known good things as conditional things, rewards at the end of a measurement. Out there I learned that some good things are unconditional for the dumbest and most beautiful reason possible: because nobody is in charge of distributing them.

In certain places, with certain plants, I also got brief, strange access to the part of myself that had learned to leave the room from the inside. The doors I had built years earlier for protection, the ones from the second chapter of this story, opened a little, and I finally got to look at what had been sitting behind them the whole time.

I had always assumed it was something broken, some damaged machinery I would need to apologize for. It was not. Behind the doors was someone mostly intact who had simply been waiting a very long time. And that gave me a distinction I now think about constantly: I had not been empty. I had been away. Empty and away look identical from the outside, but they are completely different problems with completely different solutions, and confusing the two had quietly cost me years.

Feeling has been coming back since then, and I want to describe the pace honestly, because this is the part of stories like this that usually gets a montage. There is no montage. It is slow and it is unreliable. Some days I still watch my own life from a slight distance, like the film is playing in the next room.

Other days something completely ordinary, a meal, a road, a person laughing at something stupid, lands at full volume, and I notice that I am actually here for it, and the noticing itself feels brand new. Someone loves me now, which my older internal software has strong opinions about. The part of me that concluded long ago that I was unlovable is very old, very confident, and updated its beliefs approximately never.

But she stays. And she stays. And evidence has this annoying property where it accumulates even in brains that are actively resisting it. So I have started handling that old conclusion the way I handle any other claim in my work: show me the data. It was drawn from a tiny sample, by a child, under terrible conditions.

In any other context I would have thrown a result like that out years ago. It should never have been carried this far without being checked.

Here is where the two halves of this story turn out to be the same story. I spent years building ways out: out of rooms, out of a test's verdict, out of other people's software, out of other people's definitions of what my work was supposed to be. Building a way back in, into my own life, on purpose, at full volume, is harder than every one of those projects, mostly for one annoying reason: I cannot do it from behind glass.

There is no View Source for this one. But the good news, and I do think it is good news, is that the method transfers. Start with whatever machine is in front of you, however slow it is. Learn its actual rules instead of the ones you were handed. Make something small and real. Then make the next thing. That method got a kid with a GPRS connection and a borrowed computer all the way to simulated nervous systems.

I have time to learn this one the same way. And I am, at this point, extremely good at building. This is, when you zoom all the way out, just one more thing being built.

One last piece of bookkeeping. I opened this site's repository in September 2017 with the description "My website to tell my story," which, credit where due, was accurate advertising from a guy whose entire deployment pipeline at the time was typing python bot.py on a phone. The machines got faster. The problems moved from cheat codes and copied HTML all the way to compilers, GPU kernels, and whole-brain connectomes. But the bias stayed exactly where a slow Pentium 4 installed it sometime around 2013: keep the footprint small, read the source, and own the whole stack, from the bytes up.
